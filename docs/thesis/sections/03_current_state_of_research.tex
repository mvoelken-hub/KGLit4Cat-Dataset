\label{chap:sota}

The following chapter reviews the current state of research that motivates an \ac{LLM}-supported workflow for semantic metadata extraction from catalytic experiments.
First, the current approach of catalysis research to the \ac{FAIR} principles will be examined \cite{wilkinson2016fair,roccaserra2023faircookbook}.
After that, semantic metadata standards and resources are presented, including both application profiles and the vocabularies and ontologies that can provide term-level grounding.
Furthermore, the chapter addresses and discusses how \ac{NLP} and \ac{LLM} workflows already extract structured knowledge from scientific sources.
The final section then identifies the operational gap that leads into the goals for the workflow developed.

\section{FAIR Data Practice in Catalysis}
\label{sec:fair-data-practice-catalysis}


Catalysis research produces heterogeneous experimental and computational data that differ both in structure and in the context in which they are used.
Currently, this data and its associated metadata are often fragmented across local research environments and publication channels, which limits machine-readable exchange and later reuse \cite{wulf2021unified,mendes2025data}.
Catalysis research produces heterogeneous data across publications, instrument exports, local folders, electronic laboratory systems, and repository deposits.
Related work in chemistry and materials science similarly calls for a transition from spreadsheet-based data handling toward database-backed FAIR infrastructures in which research data are comprehensively described and made available for analysis and reuse \cite{duke2022datastorage,scheffler2022fair}.
Recent discussions of digital catalysis describe the \ac{FAIR} principles as a necessary response to this fragmentation, because available data often lacks the contextual metadata needed for discovery, interpretation, comparison, and later computational use \cite{wilkinson2016fair,marshall2023digital,mendes2021opendata,mendes2025data}.

\subsection{Findability}
\label{sec:fair-findability}

Under the \ac{FAIR} principles, findability depends on globally unique and persistent identifiers and registration in searchable resources \cite{wilkinson2016fair}.
DataCite contributes to resource-level findability by associating research data and other research outputs with a DOI and a standardized metadata record.
Its mandatory properties identify the resource, its creators, title, publisher, publication year, and type, while recommended properties such as subjects, descriptions, and related identifiers improve discovery and linking \cite{datacite2026metadata}.
This primarily improves resource-level findability.
A dataset can be cited, resolved, indexed, and linked to related publications, software, or other research objects.
For catalysis, however, this does not by itself make the scientific content of a dataset searchable at the level of catalyst composition, reaction conditions, pretreatment, analytical method, or kinetic property.

Content-level findability requires domain metadata and repository-side search functions in addition to persistent identification.
Repo4Cat is designed as a catalysis repository for publishing and discovering datasets with catalysis-specific requirements \cite{kushnarenko2025repo4cat}.
The \ac{NOMAD} catalysis plugin goes further toward domain search by supporting structured upload and queries over reactions, catalyst materials, reaction conditions, and kinetic properties, while aligning metadata with catalysis vocabulary resources \cite{schumann2025nomad}.

\subsection{Accessibility}
\label{sec:fair-accessibility}

Accessibility concerns whether data and metadata can be retrieved through a clear and standardized procedure.
In terms of FAIR data principles, this does not necessarily mean that all data must be openly downloadable.
Restricted access, authentication, and permission-based workspaces can still be compatible with FAIR practice when the access conditions and protocols are explicit and machine-actionable \cite{wilkinson2016fair,peters2023fair}.
This distinction matters for industrial collaborations, unpublished measurements, and sensitive laboratory data that may require controlled access before publication.
In a commercial setting, such control can be desirable because certain data may encode intellectual property or commercially relevant know-how, and adjacent industrial FAIR work treats governance, stewardship, culture, and technology as part of implementation rather than as external concerns \cite{wise2019fair,peters2023fair}.
It also motivates infrastructures in which data processing and metadata generation can run within local or institutionally controlled services, before selected records are exposed through external repositories or public catalogues \cite{kushnarenko2025repo4cat,scheffler2022fair}.


Repository infrastructures provide the main access layer.
Repo4Cat contributes storage, landing pages, publication workflows, APIs, and private working spaces for collaborative or protected data handling \cite{kushnarenko2025repo4cat}.
\ac{NOMAD} provides browser-based and machine-oriented access to structured catalysis entries and metadata, making uploaded experimental data available through a platform that can support later search, inspection, and reuse \cite{scheffler2022fair,schumann2025nomad}.
Local laboratory infrastructures address the same principle at an earlier stage of the data lifecycle: Moshantaf et al.
connect automated acquisition, standardized analysis, database upload, relationship generation, and API-based exchange for catalytic testing data \cite{duke2022datastorage,moshantaf2024advancing}.
Together, these examples show that accessibility is implemented through repository, platform, and laboratory services rather than through metadata vocabulary alone.

\subsection{Interoperability}
\label{sec:fair-interoperability}

Interoperability requires shared machine-readable representations and semantics.
Controlled vocabularies and ontologies are necessary here because they provide stable identifiers, labels, definitions, synonyms, and relations for concepts that would otherwise remain ambiguous strings.
The \ac{Voc4Cat} provides catalysis-specific terminology in \ac{SKOS} and is used in \ac{NFDI4Cat} contexts to align catalysis metadata with shared community concepts \cite{nfdi4cat2024whitepaper,david_linke_2023_8313340}.
More specialized resources provide finer-grained semantics where a broad catalysis vocabulary is insufficient: nmrCV supports NMR-specific descriptions associated with nmrML, and QUDT represents quantity kinds, units, dimensions, and datatypes \cite{peters2023fair,nmrmlSpecification,qudt2022schema}.

Vocabularies alone, however, do not define the complete structure of a dataset.
A vocabulary can identify a concept such as reaction temperature, but a metadata or data schema must define where that temperature belongs, which entity or activity it describes, what datatype it has, and which unit is attached.
\ac{ChemDCAT-AP} therefore contributes strongly to interoperability because it combines \ac{DCAT-AP}-compatible dataset description with chemistry- and catalysis-specific classes, provenance patterns, attributes, ontology references, and LinkML-based validation artifacts \cite{stroemert2026chemdcat,chemdcatapDocumentation,linkmlDocumentation}.

\subsection{Reusability}
\label{sec:fair-reusability}

Reusability is the most context-dependent FAIR dimension.
A dataset is reusable only when a later user or machine process can understand what the data represent, how they were generated, whether they may be used, and whether their quality is sufficient for the intended analysis \cite{roccaserra2023faircookbook,peters2023fair}.
\ac{ChemDCAT-AP} supports this by describing datasets, chemical entities, reactions, analytical methods, attributes, and provenance in a machine-actionable structure that can be validated through generated artifacts such as JSON Schema and SHACL shapes \cite{stroemert2026chemdcat,chemdcatapDocumentation}.
Nevertheless, \ac{ChemDCAT-AP} does not provide reusability alone.

Latest efforts in catalysis infrastructure work therefore point toward layered reusability.
The \ac{NOMAD} catalysis plugin transforms catalysis related data uploads into a structured platform representation with domain search and vocabulary alignment, making the actual experimental content more computationally usable than opaque file attachment alone \cite{schumann2025nomad}.
Local FAIR infrastructure connects acquisition, analysis, database upload, relationships, and API exchange, so that data are reusable earlier in the laboratory workflow \cite{moshantaf2024advancing}.
Data and metadata quality work in \ac{NFDI4Cat} further emphasizes use-case-driven information collection and semantic representation \cite{huskova2025improvement}.
Benchmark resources such as CatTestHub illustrate the downstream value of curated, comparable catalysis data for comparing catalysts across probe chemistries and performance metrics \cite{burte2025cattesthub}.
The practical conclusion is that FAIR catalysis data practice requires a stack of resources: persistent identifiers for discovery, repositories for access, vocabularies and schemas for interpretation, and provenance, licensing, validation, and data quality controls for reuse \cite{roccaserra2023faircookbook,duke2022datastorage,scheffler2022fair}.

\section{Semantic Metadata Standards and Resources}
\label{sec:semantic-metadata-standards-resources}

Semantic metadata modelling requires both structural specifications and shared semantic resources.
Application profiles define how metadata classes and properties are used in a particular context, including their relations, cardinalities, and value ranges.
Vocabulary resources complement this structure by providing shared identifiers, definitions, and semantic relations for domain concepts such as analytical methods, material roles, quantity kinds, units, and spectroscopy-specific parameters \cite{scheffler2022fair,peters2023fair}.
This section first introduces the application profiles used to organize chemistry- and catalysis-related metadata and then examines the vocabulary resources used to classify their central shapes.

\subsection{DCAT-AP+ and ChemDCAT-AP}
\label{sec:dcat-ap-plus-chemdcat-ap}

The design of \ac{DCAT-AP+} is based on a separation between application-profile constraints and ontology semantics.
Its \ac{LinkML} classes and slots define usage-specific node and property shapes, while their \texttt{class\_uri} and \texttt{slot\_uri} mappings refer to classes and properties from external vocabularies.
Consequently, inheritance between LinkML elements specializes their structure and validation constraints but does not itself introduce \ac{OWL} subclass or subproperty axioms.
Multiple shapes can therefore constrain different uses of the same ontology class, while derived domain profiles may retain the generic mappings or replace them with more specific domain terms \cite{dcatapplusDocumentation}.

Within this framework, \ac{DCAT-AP+} defines four central design patterns, which are visualized in \cref{fig:dcat-ap-plus}.
The first is a provenance core that extends the otherwise underspecified use of \ac{PROV-O} in \ac{DCAT-AP}.
A \texttt{dcat:Dataset} is connected through \texttt{prov:wasGeneratedBy} to a \texttt{DataGeneratingActivity}, which is a usage-specific shape mapped to \texttt{prov:Activity}.
The underlying activity pattern can describe entities that were used or generated, preceding activities that informed the activity, and agentic entities involved in carrying it out.
The more specific \texttt{DataGeneratingActivity} additionally identifies an \texttt{EvaluatedEntity} or \texttt{EvaluatedActivity} representing what was examined during data generation.
Physical instruments and software are represented through the \texttt{AgenticEntity} shapes \texttt{Device} and \texttt{Software}, respectively.
Dataset-level relations to the evaluated subject matter are additionally represented through \texttt{dcterms:subject}, allowing both dataset-centric searches for data about an entity or activity and provenance-centric searches for activities that evaluated it \cite{dcatapplusDocumentation,stroemert2026chemdcat}.

The provenance pattern also supports data-processing chains in which one dataset is derived from another.
A \texttt{DataAnalysis} represents the analytical activity, an \texttt{AnalysisSourceData} represents the source data used by that activity, and an \texttt{AnalysisDataset} represents its resulting dataset.
These elements preserve the connection between primary data generation and subsequent analysis without introducing separate ontology classes, because they remain specialized application-profile shapes mapped to \texttt{prov:Activity}, \texttt{prov:Entitiy}, and \texttt{dcat:Dataset} \cite{dcatapplusDocumentation}.

The second pattern provides generic descriptions of quantitative and qualitative attributes.
Numerical metadata is represented by \texttt{QuantitativeAttribute}.
This shape is mapped to \texttt{qudt:Quantity} and combines a value with a \texttt{qudt:QuantityKind} and, where applicable, a \texttt{qudt:Unit}.
Non-numerical metadata is represented by \texttt{QualitativeAttribute}, which is mapped to \texttt{prov:Entity} and records values such as instrument settings, identifiers, or categorical observations.
Both attribute types can be attached to entities, activities, devices, or software through a generic relation.
They represent recorded metadata values rather than complete ontological models of the underlying physical qualities, thereby providing a compact structure that remains directly queryable and comparatively simple to populate.
Domain profiles can specialize these generic attribute relations with narrower ranges and more precise predicates where suitable domain terms exist \cite{dcatapplusDocumentation}.

The third pattern is flexible classification through the \texttt{ClassifierMixin}, which adds both \texttt{rdf\_type} and \texttt{type} to the central DCAT-AP+ shapes.
The \texttt{rdf\_type} slot maps to \texttt{rdf:type} and expresses that an instance belongs to a class from a formal ontology, thereby enabling ontology-based querying and inference.
In contrast, \texttt{type} maps to \texttt{dcterms:type} and provides lightweight categorization using a controlled-vocabulary term, such as a \ac{SKOS} concept, without asserting formal class membership.
This distinction allows domain-specific semantics to be added directly to individual instances without requiring a separate \ac{OWL} subclass for every type of activity, entity, instrument, or attribute \cite{dcatapplusDocumentation}.

The fourth pattern provides optional contextual metadata through the \texttt{Plan} and \texttt{Surrounding} shapes.
A \texttt{Plan}, mapped to \texttt{prov:Plan}, represents directive information such as a method, protocol, or standard operating procedure and is linked to a \texttt{DataGeneratingActivity} through \texttt{realized\_plan}.
A \texttt{Surrounding}, mapped to \texttt{prov:Location}, represents the spatial context in which data generation occurred, such as a laboratory, field site, or computational environment, and is linked through \texttt{occurred\_in}.
These shapes are intentionally minimal and can be classified or extended by domain profiles.
The location of data generation is thereby distinguished from the geographical coverage of the resulting dataset, which remains described through the corresponding \ac{DCAT-AP} spatial metadata \cite{dcatapplusDocumentation}.

\ac{ChemDCAT-AP} is the first domain-specific application profile built on \ac{DCAT-AP+} and provides a shared metadata foundation for chemistry- and catalysis-related research data \cite{stroemert2026chemdcat,chemdcatapDocumentation}.
It imports the generic provenance, attribute, classification, and contextual patterns of \ac{DCAT-AP+} and specializes them through \ac{LinkML} inheritance.
ChemDCAT-AP classes extend the corresponding DCAT-AP+ shapes through \texttt{is\_a}, while domain-specific slots specialize generic relations in the same manner.
Their mappings refer to established semantic resources including \ac{ChEBI}, \ac{CHEMINF}, the Semanticscience Integrated Ontology, and the Named Reaction Ontology.
This preserves the structural compatibility of the generic profile while producing more precise \ac{RDF} and narrower validation constraints for chemical metadata.

The profile is organized into modules for material entities, chemical entities, chemical reactions, and their integration with the dataset and data-generation patterns of \ac{DCAT-AP+}.
The generic \texttt{EvaluatedEntity} is specialized into shapes such as \texttt{MaterialSample} and \texttt{SubstanceSample}, whereas \texttt{ChemicalReaction} specializes \texttt{EvaluatedActivity}.
Roles involved in a reaction are represented through shapes such as \texttt{StartingMaterial}, \texttt{Reagent}, \texttt{ChemicalProduct}, \texttt{Catalyst}, and \texttt{Reactor}.
At the catalogue level, convenience shapes such as \texttt{SubstanceSampleCharacterizationDataset} and \texttt{ReactionMonitoringDataset} constrain a dataset and its generating activity to the corresponding chemical subject matter \cite{stroemert2026chemdcat,chemdcatapDocumentation}.

ChemDCAT-AP also specializes the generic attribute and provenance relations of \ac{DCAT-AP+}.
Instead of representing every chemical identifier through a generic \texttt{QualitativeAttribute} and an additional \texttt{rdf\_type}, dedicated slots such as \texttt{inchi}, \texttt{inchikey}, \texttt{smiles}, and \texttt{molecular\_formula} encode their meaning directly in the schema.
Similarly, quantitative slots such as \texttt{has\_temperature}, \texttt{has\_mass}, \texttt{has\_concentration}, and \texttt{has\_yield} specialize the generic quantitative-attribute relation.
Reaction-specific relations further distinguish the roles of inputs, outputs, and participating agents.
For example, a catalyst or generated product can be described through a domain-specific slot while remaining interpretable as a specialization of the corresponding generic DCAT-AP+ provenance relation.
ChemDCAT-AP thereby shifts recurring domain semantics from repeated instance-level classification into the application-profile schema, resulting in more concise instance data and more specific validation.

ChemDCAT-AP remains a foundational rather than exhaustive reporting profile.
Its generic chemical dataset and activity shapes are intended to be further constrained by use-case-specific profiles that define required properties, cardinalities, and controlled terms for particular experimental methods or subdomains.
This extension mechanism has been demonstrated by formalizing the MARGARITAS minimum-information recommendations for \ac{NMR} spectroscopy and by applying the resulting model within the NFDI4Chem Search Service.
For catalysis, ChemDCAT-AP therefore provides reusable representations of materials, samples, reactions, catalysts, reactors, conditions, and measured quantities upon which a dedicated catalysis minimum-information profile can impose more specific reporting requirements \cite{stroemert2026chemdcat,chemdcatapDocumentation}.

\subsection{Vocabulary Resources}
\label{sec:vocabulary-resources}

Application profiles and vocabularies contribute different parts of semantic metadata modelling.
The profile defines the structure of a record and vocabulary resources then provide stable identifiers for the terms used inside that structure, such as the analytical method, a quantity kind, an admissible unit, or a specific NMR acquisition parameter.
The following resources therefore provide term-level semantics for the subsequent construction and semantic grounding of domain-specific metadata profiles.

\subsubsection{Voc4Cat}
\label{sec:voc4cat}

\ac{Voc4Cat} is a community-maintained \ac{SKOS} vocabulary developed within \ac{NFDI4Cat} for catalysis-related disciplines.
It assigns stable, resolvable \acp{URI} to catalysis concepts and provides preferred labels, alternative labels, definitions, and hierarchical relations.
These identifiers allow metadata schemas and annotations to reference shared concepts instead of representing the same terminology through independently defined local strings \cite{david_linke_2023_8313340,nfdi4cat2024whitepaper}.
As a \ac{SKOS} resource, Voc4Cat is suited to lightweight classification, indexing, and terminology-based retrieval.
A metadata record can reference a Voc4Cat concept as the value of an annotation or classification property without thereby asserting that the described resource is an instance of a corresponding \ac{OWL} class \cite{w3c2009skos}.

A simple example is the representation of a characterization method.
Voc4Cat identifies \texttt{nuclear magnetic resonance spectroscopy} through the concept \texttt{voc4cat\_0000073}, which is related as a narrower concept to \texttt{characterization technique} (\texttt{voc4cat\_0000066}).
A metadata extraction workflow can therefore map lexical variants such as \texttt{NMR} and \texttt{nuclear magnetic resonance spectroscopy} to a shared concept identifier.
More specific expressions such as \texttt{\textsuperscript{1}H NMR} should only be mapped to this generic concept when the metadata model does not preserve the more specific nucleus-dependent method.
Within the NFDI4Cat framework for ontology-based research data management, domain-specific vocabularies and standardized metadata provide the semantic basis for constructing knowledge graphs.
The white paper illustrates the use of this framework for literature search, experimental and simulation data entry, analysis, storage, and research planning \cite{nfdi4cat2024whitepaper}.

\subsubsection{Quantities, Units, Dimensions and Types Ontology (QUDT)}
\label{sec:qudt}

The \acf{QUDT} provides a cross-domain semantic model for quantities, quantity kinds, units of measure, dimensions, numerical values, and their machine-readable representations.
It complements domain vocabularies such as Voc4Cat by providing the identifiers and relations needed to express the quantitative semantics of a measurement that is associated with a characterization technique or an attribute.
A literal value such as \texttt{400} becomes explicit and machine-actionable when it is linked, for example, to \texttt{quantitykind:Frequency} and \texttt{unit:MegaHZ} \cite{qudt2022schema}.

The  \ac{QUDT} model makes these links explicit as an example shows.
The \ac{QUDT} quantity-kind vocabulary defines \texttt{quantitykind:Frequency} as the number of occurrences of a repeating event per unit time and assigns it the dimension vector \(\mathrm{T}^{-1}\).
Through \texttt{qudt:applicableUnit}, it is linked to compatible units from the \ac{QUDT} unit vocabulary, such as \texttt{unit:HZ} and \texttt{unit:MegaHZ}.
The corresponding unit resources are, in turn, associated with the frequency quantity kind through \texttt{qudt:hasQuantityKind} \cite{qudt2022schema}.
These relations can be used by validation and transformation software for unit--quantity-kind compatibility checks and conversions to a selected reference unit.
For example, an NMR spectrometer frequency of \texttt{400 MHz} can be represented using \texttt{quantitykind:Frequency} and \texttt{unit:MegaHZ} and normalized to \texttt{400,000,000 Hz} without changing the quantity being described.
Conversely, a linking of a value to a quantity kind and unit that are incompatible can be flagged as an error.
\ac{QUDT} provides the semantic relations and conversion parameters required for these operations, while their execution requires corresponding validation or transformation rules \cite{qudt2022schema}.

\subsubsection{NMR Controlled Vocabulary (nmrCV)}
\label{sec:nmr-cv}

The \ac{nmrCV} is the \ac{OWL}-based controlled vocabulary accompanying \ac{nmrML}, an open, vendor-neutral \ac{XML} format for exchanging and storing \ac{NMR} data.
While the \ac{nmrML} schema defines the document structure, \ac{nmrCV} supplies standardized identifiers for spectroscopy-specific concepts concerning instrumentation and acquisition, spectral data processing, and the description or assignment of spectral results.
It therefore provides a level of NMR-specific detail that complements broader domain vocabularies such as Voc4Cat \cite{nmrmlSpecification}.
While \ac{nmrCV} technically is an \ac{OWL} ontology, it is intended to be used as a controlled vocabulary rather than as a formal ontology with axioms and reasoning.

The scope of nmrCV extends across the NMR data lifecycle.
Instrument and acquisition context can be described through terms such as \texttt{NMR probe}, \texttt{dwell time}, and \texttt{ninety degree pulse duration}.
The vocabulary also distinguishes raw or processed data products and processing operations, including \texttt{FID data set}, \texttt{peak-picked spectrum}, and \texttt{baseline correction}.
Terms for bin boundaries, bin areas, and compound concentrations further support the description of derived or quantified spectral results.
nmrCV can therefore identify not only how a spectrum was acquired, but also the processing state and interpretation of the resulting data \cite{nmrmlSpecification}.


\section{Text Mining and LLM-Based Extraction Workflows}
\label{sec:text-mining-llm-extraction-workflows}

The application profiles and vocabularies discussed above define how catalysis metadata can be represented, but much of the information required to populate these structures remains available only in unstructured or semi-structured form.
Within scientific publications, relevant information may be distributed across prose, tables, and figures and must be integrated at the document level \cite{schilling2025text,Fan2024OpenChemIE}.
The experimental data packages addressed in this work additionally include supplementary files, reports, instrument exports, and locally maintained files.
Constructing semantic metadata is therefore not only a modelling task but also an acquisition and information-integration task.
Reviews of \ac{LLM}-based chemical data extraction describe workflows that span source collection, document parsing, structured extraction, and quality assurance \cite{schilling2025text}.

Earlier chemistry-aware and domain-adapted \ac{NLP} systems provide the methodological baseline for current \ac{LLM}-based extraction workflows. ChemDataExtractor combines chemical named-entity recognition, rule-based parsing grammars, and document-level processing to extract chemical entities together with associated properties, measurements, and relations from scientific documents \cite{swain2016chemdataextractor}. A catalysis-specific workflow was developed by Zhang et al.\ through article relevance classification, active-learning-assisted annotation, span-based entity extraction, and entity normalization. It distinguishes catalysts, reactions, reactants, products, characterization methods, and catalyst treatments and uses the extracted entities in an entity-aware search engine and a co-occurrence-based correlation analysis system \cite{zhang2022knowledge}. The study does not perform explicit semantic relation extraction between these entities.

Domain-specific pretraining further improves the processing of specialized scientific terminology. SciBERT outperforms general-domain BERT on several scientific language-processing tasks, while MatSciBERT achieves additional improvements by pretraining specifically on materials-science literature \cite{beltagy2019scibert,gupta2022matscibert}. The catalysis extraction workflow of Zhang et al.\ similarly reports gains from adapting its language model to a chemical literature corpus \cite{zhang2022knowledge}. CataLM represents a related but distinct development: it adapts a generative language model to electrocatalytic materials for knowledge exploration and catalyst-design assistance rather than evaluating a dedicated extraction pipeline \cite{wang2024catalm}. Together, these studies indicate that scientific and domain-specific pretraining can provide measurable advantages when terminology, notation, and entity distinctions differ from general-language corpora.

Generative \ac{LLM}s expand information extraction from the prediction of predefined entity labels toward the generation of structured records governed by an explicit target representation. Dagdelen et al.\ fine-tune pretrained language models for joint entity and relation extraction in materials chemistry, producing records from sentences or paragraphs in formats including structured JSON objects \cite{dagdelen2024structured}. SPIRES follows a different, zero-shot approach: it recursively interrogates an \ac{LLM} according to a user-defined nested schema and grounds extracted entities against external ontologies and controlled vocabularies \cite{caufield2024spires}. CatMiner applies related ideas to heterogeneous catalysis by extracting user-specified structure--environment--property records through sequential prompting, domain-specific instructions, follow-up checks, and searches for relevant context across sentences and paragraphs \cite{walls2025catminer}. Its scope is primarily textual, despite addressing literature on heterogeneous catalysis rather than heterogeneous document modalities.

EDC addresses the related task of constructing knowledge graphs from text through three separate phases: open information extraction, schema definition, and post-extraction canonicalization. It can operate with either a predefined target schema or a schema induced from the extracted information and retrieves relevant schema elements when the complete schema is too large for the prompt \cite{zhang2024edc}. Together, these approaches illustrate several distinct forms of generative information extraction: supervised structured generation, zero-shot schema population, domain-specific iterative extraction, and schema-aware canonicalization. They extend rather than replace conventional entity- and relation-extraction methods \cite{xu2024generativeie}.

Knowledge graphs primarily occur in extraction workflows as target representations, while ontologies, controlled vocabularies, and schemas provide constraints and canonical identifiers for constructing them. Extracted entities and relations can thereby be transformed from document-specific expressions into normalized, queryable representations that connect catalysts, reactions, materials, conditions, and reported observations \cite{diaz2025knowledgegraphs}. SPIRES demonstrates a schema-guided form of this process by recursively populating a user-defined knowledge schema and grounding extracted elements against identifiers from existing ontologies and vocabularies \cite{caufield2024spires}.

A catalysis-specific workflow by Behr et al.\ combines named-entity recognition based on CatalysisIE with ontology matching and ontology learning. Extracted entities are compared with existing semantic resources, incorporated into domain knowledge graphs, and made accessible through predefined \ac{SPARQL} queries \cite{behr2024knowledgegraphs}. Oarga et al.\ follow a different approach by using open \ac{LLM}s to extract a domain vocabulary, induce a taxonomy and relation schema, and instantiate an ontology and knowledge graph from scientific literature. Their evaluation includes an application to single-atom-catalyst literature \cite{oarga2026scientifickg}. This approach generates a knowledge model from the source corpus, whereas schema-guided workflows such as SPIRES project extracted information onto an externally specified model.

Knowledge-graph construction also requires an infrastructure layer beyond information extraction. KG-Hub illustrates a modular extract--transform--load architecture for ontology integration, versioned graph construction, standardized exchange, and the reuse of transformed subgraphs \cite{caufield2023kghub}. Although developed for biological knowledge graphs rather than catalysis extraction, it demonstrates how extracted and normalized records can be incorporated into reproducible graph-building workflows.

Semantic Web technologies and knowledge graphs appear in this literature both as targets and as supports for extraction.
Extracted entities and relations can populate knowledge graphs that connect catalysts, reactions, materials, conditions, and performance observations \cite{diaz2025knowledgegraphs}.
SPIRES uses ontology grounding while populating knowledge bases, and open LLM pipelines for scientific knowledge graph and ontology generation show that vocabulary extraction, taxonomy construction, and graph population can be combined in one workflow \cite{caufield2024spires,oarga2026scientifickg}.
KG-Hub illustrates the more general infrastructure pattern of modular knowledge graph construction and exchange \cite{caufield2023kghub}.
The reviewed literature supports knowledge graphs and ontology-grounded extraction as important directions, but graph database technologies such as Neo4j should be treated as implementation choices unless a specific study reports them directly.

Reliability remains the central limitation of LLM-based extraction.
Retrieval-augmented generation connects model outputs to external evidence, and RAG surveys distinguish increasingly modular retrieval and generation architectures \cite{lewis2020retrieval,gao2023ragsurvey}.
Long-context studies caution that simply placing more text in the prompt does not guarantee faithful use of evidence, especially when relevant passages appear in the middle of long contexts \cite{liu2024lostmiddle}.
Structured-output methods address a different failure mode: grammar-constrained decoding can enforce formal output validity, while Generate-and-Organize prompting separates content generation from output formatting \cite{geng2023grammar,li2024structuredoutput}.
For scientific extraction, these findings support a layered workflow: retrieve source evidence, generate candidate content, serialize it into a declared structure, ground terms against vocabularies, validate the result, and keep provenance available for human review \cite{schilling2025text}.

\section{Use Cases for Extracted Metadata}
\label{sec:use-cases-extracted-metadata}

Extracted metadata is valuable because it enables downstream use, not because structured records are an end in themselves.
One major use case is knowledge graph population for catalyst discovery.
Knowledge graphs can connect catalysts, supports, synthesis procedures, reaction conditions, characterization methods, performance values, and literature provenance in a queryable structure \cite{diaz2025knowledgegraphs}.
Broader surveys of scientific knowledge graphs describe a vision in which such graphs become infrastructure for AI systems and can co-evolve with LLM-based discovery workflows \cite{ding2026scikg}.
For catalysis, this means that extraction workflows should produce records that can be traced, normalized, and inserted into graph or repository structures rather than only summarized for human reading.

A second use case is FAIR repository population.
Repository systems such as Repo4Cat and NOMAD need structured metadata to make datasets findable, interpretable, and reusable across projects and institutions \cite{kushnarenko2025repo4cat,scheffler2022fair,schumann2025nomad}.
Local FAIR data infrastructures show how laboratory acquisition, analysis, database storage, and repository-level exchange can be connected through APIs and standardized procedures \cite{duke2022datastorage,moshantaf2024advancing}.
Extraction workflows can support this ecosystem by helping researchers create repository-ready metadata from heterogeneous material before or during dataset deposit.

A third use case is AI-guided catalyst optimization and benchmarking.
Bayesian optimization with in-context learning and uncertainty-calibrated LLM embeddings shows that natural-language or embedding-based descriptions can be used in optimization settings, but these approaches still depend on meaningful representations of experiments and outcomes \cite{ramos2025boicl,rankovic2025uncertainty}.
Benchmark resources such as CatTestHub demonstrate the importance of curated, comparable catalyst data for evaluating and comparing performance across conditions and catalyst classes \cite{burte2025cattesthub}.
Normalized metadata can improve such workflows because values, units, catalyst descriptions, and reaction contexts become comparable rather than remaining ad hoc strings.

A fourth use case is integration with automated or closed-loop laboratories.
Local catalysis data infrastructures already connect automated acquisition, standardized analysis, and database upload, while autonomous experimentation literature points toward design--build--test--learn cycles in which machine-readable data are essential \cite{duke2022datastorage,moshantaf2024advancing,weigmann2026autonomous}.
Catalyst informatics makes the same point from the data-driven design perspective: predictive and optimization workflows require structured descriptions of catalyst composition, preparation, operating conditions, and performance \cite{takahashi2023catalysts}.
Metadata extraction does not itself implement a closed-loop laboratory, but it can provide one of the prerequisite layers: converting heterogeneous experimental documentation into structured, grounded, reusable records.

\section{Research Gap}
\label{sec:research-gap}

The literature reviewed in this chapter shows that the central gap is not a lack of individual building blocks.
Catalysis research already has FAIR infrastructure initiatives, repository concepts, metadata profiles, vocabularies, ontology surveys, knowledge graph methods, and increasingly capable text-mining and LLM extraction systems.
NFDI4Cat, NFDI4Chem, Repo4Cat, NOMAD, ChemDCAT-AP, Voc4Cat, Ontologies4Cat, CatalysisIE, SPIRES, CatMiner, and recent LLM-based knowledge graph approaches all address important parts of the problem \cite{nfdi4cat2024whitepaper,steinbeck2023nfdi4chem,kushnarenko2025repo4cat,schumann2025nomad,stroemert2026chemdcat,veluvali2025bridging,behr2024ontologies4cat,zhang2022knowledge,caufield2024spires,walls2025catminer,oarga2026scientifickg}.
What remains less developed is an operational workflow that connects these parts for heterogeneous experimental packages.

Existing extraction systems are strongest where the input is a scientific article or a controlled text corpus.
CatalysisIE, ChemDataExtractor, CatMiner, SPIRES, and general structured extraction studies provide important patterns for article selection, entity extraction, schema population, normalization, and knowledge-base construction \cite{swain2016chemdataextractor,zhang2022knowledge,caufield2024spires,dagdelen2024structured,walls2025catminer}.
Experimental data packages, however, can contain PDFs, spreadsheets, local metadata files, images, instrument exports, supplementary tables, and partially structured folders.
The information needed for a metadata profile may therefore be distributed across document types and may not follow the rhetorical structure of a journal article.

A second gap concerns trust.
Schema-valid output is necessary but not sufficient for scientific metadata.
A generated record can satisfy JSON Schema or SHACL while still assigning a value to the wrong catalyst, confusing experimental and computational results, losing unit context, or grounding a term to an inappropriate vocabulary concept.
The reviewed literature therefore points toward evidence retrieval, provenance, term grounding, validation, and human review as complementary controls rather than optional additions \cite{schilling2025text,liu2024lostmiddle,geng2023grammar,li2024structuredoutput,diaz2025knowledgegraphs}.
The challenge is to combine these controls into a workflow that remains inspectable and practical.

This thesis is positioned in that operational gap.
It does not propose a new catalysis ontology, a new repository, or a fully automated literature database.
Instead, the following chapter develops SIMONE as a workflow that connects heterogeneous source handling, evidence-oriented extraction, metadata profile projection, vocabulary grounding, validation, and human review.
The contribution is therefore not the replacement of existing standards or vocabularies, but the investigation of how they can be made usable as constraints and grounding resources in an LLM-supported metadata extraction process.
