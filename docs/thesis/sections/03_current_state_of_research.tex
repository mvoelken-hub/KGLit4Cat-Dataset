\label{chap:sota}

The following chapter reviews the current state of research that motivates an LLM-supported workflow for semantic metadata extraction from catalytic experiments.
First, it will be examined how catalysis research currently approaches FAIR data practice.
After that, semantic metadata standards and resources are presented, including both application profiles and the vocabularies, taxonomies, and ontologies that provide term-level grounding.
Furthermore, the chapter addresses and discusses how \ac{NLP} and \ac{LLM} workflows already extract structured knowledge from scientific sources.
The final section then identifies the operational gap that leads into the goals for the workflow developed.

\section{FAIR Data Practice in Catalysis}
\label{sec:fair-data-practice-catalysis}

Catalysis research produces heterogeneous data across publications, supplementary information, instrument exports, local folders, electronic laboratory systems, and repository deposits.
Recent discussions of digital catalysis describe the \ac{FAIR} principles as a necessary response to this fragmentation, because available data often lacks the contextual metadata needed for discovery, interpretation, comparison, and later computational use \cite{wilkinson2016fair,marshall2023digital,mendes2021opendata,mendes2025data}.
The four FAIR principles should not be read as four isolated technical layers.
The same infrastructure component can support several of them.
A repository can make a dataset findable and accessible, a vocabulary can improve interoperability and search, and a metadata profile can support both semantic exchange and later reuse.
\cref{tab:fair-catalysis-resource-map} therefore gives an orientation map rather than a strict one-to-one assignment.

\subsection{Findability}
\label{sec:fair-findability}

Findability begins with stable identification and descriptive metadata.
DataCite is relevant here because its metadata schema supports the publication and citation of research data and other research outputs through globally unique persistent identifiers, especially DOIs, together with descriptive fields such as creators, titles, publishers, publication year, subjects, descriptions, related identifiers, and resource types \cite{datacite2026metadata}.
This primarily improves resource-level findability.
A dataset can be cited, resolved, indexed, and linked to related publications, software, or other research objects.
For catalysis, however, this does not by itself make the scientific content of a dataset searchable at the level of catalyst composition, reaction conditions, pretreatment, analytical method, or kinetic property.

Content-level findability requires domain metadata and repository-side search functions in addition to persistent identification.
Repo4Cat is designed as a catalysis repository for publishing and discovering datasets with catalysis-specific requirements \cite{kushnarenko2025repo4cat}.
The \ac{NOMAD} catalysis plugin goes further toward domain search by supporting structured upload and queries over reactions, catalyst materials, reaction conditions, and kinetic properties, while aligning metadata with catalysis vocabulary resources \cite{schumann2025nomad}.

\subsection{Accessibility}
\label{sec:fair-accessibility}

Accessibility concerns whether data and metadata can be retrieved through a clear and standardized procedure.
In terms of FAIR data principles, this does not necessarily mean that all data must be openly downloadable.
Restricted access, authentication, and permission-based workspaces can still be compatible with FAIR practice when the access conditions and protocols are explicit \cite{wilkinson2016fair}.
This distinction matters for industrial collaborations, unpublished measurements, and sensitive laboratory data that may require controlled access before publication.
In a commercial setting, such control can be desirable because certain data may encode intellectual property or commercially relevant know-how.
It also motivates infrastructures in which data processing and metadata generation can run within local or institutionally controlled services, before selected records are exposed through external repositories or public catalogues \cite{kushnarenko2025repo4cat}.


Repository infrastructures provide the main access layer.
Repo4Cat contributes storage, landing pages, publication workflows, APIs, and private working spaces for collaborative or protected data handling \cite{kushnarenko2025repo4cat}.
\ac{NOMAD} provides browser-based and machine-oriented access to structured catalysis entries and metadata, making uploaded experimental data available through a platform that can support later search, inspection, and reuse \cite{schumann2025nomad}.
Local laboratory infrastructures address the same principle at an earlier stage of the data lifecycle: Moshantaf et al. connect automated acquisition, standardized analysis, database upload, relationship generation, and API-based exchange for catalytic testing data \cite{moshantaf2024advancing}.
Together, these examples show that accessibility is implemented through repository, platform, and laboratory services rather than through metadata vocabulary alone.

\subsection{Interoperability}
\label{sec:fair-interoperability}

Interoperability requires shared machine-readable representations and semantics.
Controlled vocabularies and ontologies are necessary here because they provide stable identifiers, labels, definitions, synonyms, and relations for concepts that would otherwise remain ambiguous strings.
The \ac{Voc4Cat} provides catalysis-specific terminology in \ac{SKOS} and is used in \ac{NFDI4Cat} contexts and the \ac{NOMAD} catalysis plugin to align workflow or repository metadata with shared catalysis concepts \cite{nfdi4cat2024whitepaper,schumann2025nomad}.
A MaRDIFlow extension further demonstrates how Voc4Cat can be integrated into computational workflow metadata through API-based vocabulary lookup and validation \cite{veluvali2025bridging}.
More specialized resources provide finer-grained semantics where a broad catalysis vocabulary is insufficient: CHMO supports chemical experimental and analytical method terminology, nmrCV supports NMR-specific descriptions associated with nmrML, and QUDT represents quantity kinds, units, dimensions, and datatypes \cite{chmoOboFoundry,nmrmlSpecification,qudt2022schema}.

Vocabularies alone, however, do not define the complete structure of a dataset.
A vocabulary can identify a concept such as reaction temperature, but a metadata or data schema must define where that temperature belongs, which entity or activity it describes, what datatype it has, and which unit is attached.
\ac{ChemDCAT-AP} therefore contributes strongly to interoperability because it combines \ac{DCAT-AP}-compatible dataset description with chemistry- and catalysis-specific classes, provenance patterns, attributes, ontology references, and LinkML-based validation artifacts \cite{stroemert2026chemdcat,chemdcatapDocumentation,linkmlDocumentation}.

\subsection{Reusability}
\label{sec:fair-reusability}

Reusability is the most context-dependent FAIR dimension.
A dataset is reusable only when a later user or machine process can understand what the data represent, how they were generated, whether they may be used, and whether their quality is sufficient for the intended analysis.
\ac{ChemDCAT-AP} supports this by describing datasets, chemical entities, reactions, analytical methods, attributes, and provenance in a machine-actionable structure that can be validated through generated artifacts such as JSON Schema and SHACL shapes \cite{stroemert2026chemdcat,chemdcatapDocumentation}.
Nevertheless, \ac{ChemDCAT-AP} does not provide reusability alone.

Latest efforts in catalysis infrastructure work therefore point toward layered reusability.
The \ac{NOMAD} catalysis plugin transforms catalysis related data uploads into a structured platform representation with domain search and vocabulary alignment, making the actual experimental content more computationally usable than opaque file attachment alone \cite{schumann2025nomad}.
Local FAIR infrastructure connects acquisition, analysis, database upload, relationships, and API exchange, so that data are reusable earlier in the laboratory workflow \cite{moshantaf2024advancing}.
Data and metadata quality work in \ac{NFDI4Cat} further emphasizes use-case-driven information collection and semantic representation \cite{huskova2025improvement}.
Benchmark resources such as CatTestHub illustrate the downstream value of curated, comparable catalysis data for comparing catalysts across probe chemistries and performance metrics \cite{burte2025cattesthub}.
The practical conclusion is that FAIR catalysis data practice requires a stack of resources: persistent identifiers for discovery, repositories for access, vocabularies and schemas for interpretation, and provenance, licensing, validation, and data quality controls for reuse.

\section{Semantic Metadata Standards and Resources}
\label{sec:semantic-metadata-standards-resources}

\subsection{Application Profiles}
\label{sec:application-profiles}

The most relevant current application-profile development for this work is \ac{DCAT-AP+}, a \ac{LinkML}-based extension of \ac{DCAT-AP} 3.0 that adds structured scientific context to catalogue-level dataset descriptions \cite{semic2024dcatap,dcatapplusDocumentation,stroemert2026chemdcat}.
Its practical message is to keep the base profile domain-agnostic, extend DCAT-AP only with broadly reusable terms from \ac{PROV-O}, \ac{DCTERMS}, and \ac{QUDT}, and use \ac{LinkML} as the mediator between instance data, validation artifacts, and \ac{RDF} export \cite{w3c2013provo,qudt2022schema,dcatapplusDocumentation,linkmlDocumentation}.
This puts \ac{DCAT-AP+} in a middle position between broader vocabularies
two extremes that are both problematic for day-to-day catalysis data handling.
Controlled vocabularies such as \ac{Voc4Cat} provide terminology but not enough relation structure on their own, while full ontologies can be too complex as direct reporting schemas.
\ac{DCAT-AP+} therefore acts as a compact schema layer that can map data elements to terms and still remain simple enough for software integration and profile extension.

The documentation frames the model as a way to connect a dataset to use-case-specific context: which entity or activity the dataset is about, which activity generated it, which tools, surroundings, agents, and plans were involved, and which qualitative or quantitative attributes were observed \cite{dcatapplusDocumentation}.
This makes \ac{DCAT-AP+} especially relevant for catalysis and chemistry data because the scientific value of a dataset often depends less on the catalogue title alone than on the experiment, sample, measurement, method, and measured quantities that produced the files.

The dataset remains the catalogue resource, while the added pattern makes the generating activity, evaluated entities or activities, participating agents and instruments, plans, locations, classifications, and attributes explicit. This turns a dataset record from a discoverability object into a structured description of how scientific data were produced and what they describe \cite{semic2024dcatap,w3c2013provo,dcatapplusDocumentation,stroemert2026chemdcat}.}
    \label{fig:dcat-ap-plus-schema}
\end{figure}

The central design pattern is shown in \cref{fig:dcat-ap-plus-schema}.
DCAT-AP already allows a dataset to be linked to a provenance activity, but DCAT-AP+ gives that link a concrete internal structure.
A \texttt{Dataset} can be connected through \texttt{prov:wasGeneratedBy} to an \texttt{Activity}; this activity can be classified with an ontology class, associated with agents or instruments, linked to a plan or method, and connected to evaluated entities or activities.
The evaluated objects and the generating activity can then carry attributes.
Qualitative attributes provide controlled descriptions, while quantitative attributes connect literal values to quantity kinds and units, following the QUDT-oriented pattern used in the documentation \cite{w3c2013provo,qudt2022schema,dcatapplusDocumentation}.
This pattern is the part of DCAT-AP+ that matters most for metadata extraction: it defines where extracted statements such as \enquote{sample}, \enquote{measurement}, \enquote{spectrometer}, \enquote{temperature}, \enquote{solvent}, or \enquote{reaction time} belong in a reusable graph-shaped record.

DCAT-AP+ is also important because its schema is operational rather than only descriptive.
The model is maintained as a LinkML schema and the documentation reports generated artifacts such as YAML, \ac{JSON} Schema, \ac{JSON}-LD context, \ac{SHACL} shapes, and merged schema documentation \cite{dcatapplusDocumentation,linkmlDocumentation,w3c2017shacl}.
This matters for the state of research because it connects three layers that are often treated separately: a human-readable documentation model, a machine-readable validation schema, and an \ac{RDF}-compatible semantic representation.
For an extraction workflow, this means that candidate metadata can be projected into an explicit target structure and then checked against profile-level constraints rather than remaining a collection of untyped fields.

\ac{ChemDCAT-AP} is the chemistry and catalysis specialization built from this DCAT-AP+ foundation.
The ChemDCAT-AP paper makes this layering explicit.
DCAT-AP+ is the domain-agnostic layer.
ChemDCAT-AP imports it and adds chemistry- and catalysis-specific concepts \cite{stroemert2026chemdcat,chemdcatapDocumentation}.
These additions include chemical entities, substances, samples, reactions, reagents, solvents, catalysts, analytical methods, pressures, yields, and related attribute patterns.
In the layered profile family, DCAT-AP+ carries the reusable provenance and attribute design; ChemDCAT-AP adds the restrictions and terminology mappings shared by chemistry-specific contexts; and more specific profiles, such as MIChI-oriented schemas or future catalysis extensions, can add subdomain requirements on top of that shared layer.
For catalysis, this layered design is important because it leaves room for a profile that captures minimum catalysis information, synthesis and characterization metadata, reaction context, catalyst composition, support, solvent, storage conditions, preparation method, and measured catalyst properties while still inheriting the common DCAT-AP+ and ChemDCAT-AP patterns.
ChemDCAT-AP is therefore both the origin and the practical application of the DCAT-AP+ idea for this thesis: it demonstrates that the generic base pattern can be specialized into a profile suitable for NFDI4Chem and NFDI4Cat data exchange \cite{stroemert2026chemdcat,steinbeck2023nfdi4chem,nfdi4cat2024whitepaper}.


\subsection{Semantic Metadata Resources}
\label{sec:taxonomies-vocabularies-ontologies}

Semantic metadata also requires term-level grounding. SKOS provides a model for controlled vocabularies and concept schemes, including preferred labels, alternative labels, definitions, and broader or narrower concept relations \cite{w3c2009skos}. OWL supports richer ontology modelling with class expressions and logical semantics \cite{w3c2012owl2primer}. Quantity and unit systems such as QUDT address a different part of the same problem by representing measurable quantities, quantity kinds, units, dimensions, and datatypes \cite{qudt2022schema}. For catalysis metadata, these resources play complementary roles: controlled vocabularies stabilize labels, ontologies add formal relations, and quantity vocabularies make numerical observations comparable.

QUDT is central for quantitative normalization. Catalysis records contain temperatures, pressures, flow rates, times, loadings, conversions, selectivities, yields, rates, and other measured or calculated quantities. These values are only reusable when the quantity kind and unit are made explicit and can be converted or compared across sources. ChemDCAT-AP's attribute patterns make this kind of normalization operational by allowing measured attributes to be associated with semantic identifiers rather than leaving them as isolated strings or numbers \cite{qudt2022schema,stroemert2026chemdcat}. In practice, QUDT therefore helps turn textual mentions such as temperatures or reaction times into comparable metadata.

Voc4Cat addresses the qualitative terminology problem for catalysis. Developed in the NFDI4Cat context, this SKOS vocabulary provides shared catalysis concepts, labels, definitions, and persistent identifiers \cite{nfdi4cat2024whitepaper,veluvali2025bridging}. Its value is practical as well as terminological: the MaRDIFlow extension and the NOMAD catalysis plugin show how vocabulary alignment can support semantic workflow metadata and structured repository submission \cite{veluvali2025bridging,schumann2025nomad}. Voc4Cat therefore functions as a community vocabulary that can reduce ambiguity in catalyst, reaction, method, and workflow descriptions.

More specialized vocabularies are needed when a broad catalysis vocabulary is not precise enough. The Chemical Methods Ontology provides controlled terms for chemical experimental and analytical methods, including many characterization and measurement techniques \cite{chmoOboFoundry}. nmrCV provides NMR-specific terminology associated with nmrML, supporting more precise descriptions of NMR acquisition and related concepts \cite{nmrmlSpecification}. These resources illustrate why semantic grounding cannot rely on one vocabulary alone: a catalysis dataset may need a catalysis term for the reaction context, a method ontology term for the characterization procedure, an NMR-specific term for an acquisition concept, and a QUDT identifier for the measured quantity and unit.

The broader ontology landscape is similarly distributed. Ontologies4Cat surveys and classifies ontology resources relevant to the catalysis research data value chain, because useful concepts are spread across chemical, biological, materials, process-engineering, and catalysis-specific resources \cite{behr2024ontologies4cat}. Reac4Cat extends reaction modelling with semantic reaction classification and uses established chemical resources for functional group and reaction descriptions \cite{borgelt2025ontology}. OntoCAPE represents process-engineering concepts and remains relevant where catalyst data connect to reactor and process models \cite{marquardt2010ontocape}. The state of the art is therefore not a single universal ontology, but a modular landscape in which practical workflows must choose and align the vocabularies and ontologies needed for a specific metadata task.

\section{Text Mining and LLM-Based Extraction Workflows}
\label{sec:text-mining-llm-extraction-workflows}

The standards and vocabularies discussed above define how catalysis metadata can be represented, but much of the required information remains embedded in scientific articles, supplementary information, tables, figures, reports, instrument exports, and local files. Chemical data extraction is therefore an acquisition problem as much as a modelling problem. Recent reviews of LLM-based chemical data extraction describe end-to-end workflows that move from document collection and parsing to structured output, quality assurance, and domain validation \cite{schilling2025text}. In catalysis, the extraction task is especially demanding because catalyst identity, pretreatment, reaction context, analytical method, and performance value may be distributed across several sections of a paper or across several files \cite{zhang2022knowledge,walls2025catminer}.

Classical and domain-specific NLP systems provide the baseline for this task. ChemDataExtractor showed that chemistry-aware parsing, rule-based grammars, and document-level processing can extract chemical records before the current wave of generative LLMs \cite{swain2016chemdataextractor}. CatalysisIE applies a more catalysis-specific pipeline with article selection, named entity recognition, relation extraction, and an entity-aware search interface for classes such as catalysts, reactions, reactants, products, characterization, and treatment \cite{zhang2022knowledge}. Domain adaptation also matters at the language-model level: SciBERT and MatSciBERT show that scientific and materials-domain pretraining improves representation quality for specialized text, while CataLM demonstrates the same motivation for catalyst-domain LLMs \cite{beltagy2019scibert,gupta2022matscibert,wang2024catalm}. These systems show that chemical and catalysis language differ enough from general text to justify domain-aware models and resources.

Generative LLMs extend extraction by turning structured information extraction into a schema-guided generation problem. Dagdelen et al. show that LLMs can extract structured records from scientific text when the task is formulated carefully \cite{dagdelen2024structured}. SPIRES uses structured prompt interrogation and recursive extraction to populate LinkML-style schemas, connecting LLM output to explicit knowledge models rather than only to free-form summaries \cite{caufield2024spires}. CatMiner provides a catalysis-specific example in which LLMs extract user-specified structure--environment--property data from heterogeneous catalysis literature using iterative prompting, domain knowledge injection, and document-wide context handling \cite{walls2025catminer}. EDC adds a related knowledge-graph construction pattern by separating extraction, schema definition, and canonicalization \cite{zhang2024edc}. These approaches show that the field is moving from token-level extraction toward workflows that combine prompting, schema structure, normalization, and review.

Semantic Web technologies and knowledge graphs appear in this literature both as targets and as supports for extraction. Extracted entities and relations can populate knowledge graphs that connect catalysts, reactions, materials, conditions, and performance observations \cite{diaz2025knowledgegraphs}. SPIRES uses ontology grounding while populating knowledge bases, and open LLM pipelines for scientific knowledge graph and ontology generation show that vocabulary extraction, taxonomy construction, and graph population can be combined in one workflow \cite{caufield2024spires,oarga2026scientifickg}. KG-Hub illustrates the more general infrastructure pattern of modular knowledge graph construction and exchange \cite{caufield2023kghub}. The reviewed literature supports knowledge graphs and ontology-grounded extraction as important directions, but graph database technologies such as Neo4j should be treated as implementation choices unless a specific study reports them directly.

Reliability remains the central limitation of LLM-based extraction. Retrieval-augmented generation connects model outputs to external evidence, and RAG surveys distinguish increasingly modular retrieval and generation architectures \cite{lewis2020retrieval,gao2023ragsurvey}. Long-context studies caution that simply placing more text in the prompt does not guarantee faithful use of evidence, especially when relevant passages appear in the middle of long contexts \cite{liu2024lostmiddle}. Structured-output methods address a different failure mode: grammar-constrained decoding can enforce formal output validity, while Generate-and-Organize prompting separates content generation from output formatting \cite{geng2023grammar,li2024structuredoutput}. For scientific extraction, these findings support a layered workflow: retrieve source evidence, generate candidate content, serialize it into a declared structure, ground terms against vocabularies, validate the result, and keep provenance available for human review \cite{schilling2025text}.

\section{Use Cases for Extracted Metadata}
\label{sec:use-cases-extracted-metadata}

Extracted metadata is valuable because it enables downstream use, not because structured records are an end in themselves. One major use case is knowledge graph population for catalyst discovery. Knowledge graphs can connect catalysts, supports, synthesis procedures, reaction conditions, characterization methods, performance values, and literature provenance in a queryable structure \cite{diaz2025knowledgegraphs}. Broader surveys of scientific knowledge graphs describe a vision in which such graphs become infrastructure for AI systems and can co-evolve with LLM-based discovery workflows \cite{ding2026scikg}. For catalysis, this means that extraction workflows should produce records that can be traced, normalized, and inserted into graph or repository structures rather than only summarized for human reading.

A second use case is FAIR repository population. Repository systems such as Repo4Cat and NOMAD need structured metadata to make datasets findable, interpretable, and reusable across projects and institutions \cite{kushnarenko2025repo4cat,schumann2025nomad}. Local FAIR data infrastructures show how laboratory acquisition, analysis, database storage, and repository-level exchange can be connected through APIs and standardized procedures \cite{moshantaf2024advancing}. Extraction workflows can support this ecosystem by helping researchers create repository-ready metadata from heterogeneous material before or during dataset deposit.

A third use case is AI-guided catalyst optimization and benchmarking. Bayesian optimization with in-context learning and uncertainty-calibrated LLM embeddings shows that natural-language or embedding-based descriptions can be used in optimization settings, but these approaches still depend on meaningful representations of experiments and outcomes \cite{ramos2025boicl,rankovic2025uncertainty}. Benchmark resources such as CatTestHub demonstrate the importance of curated, comparable catalyst data for evaluating and comparing performance across conditions and catalyst classes \cite{burte2025cattesthub}. Normalized metadata can improve such workflows because values, units, catalyst descriptions, and reaction contexts become comparable rather than remaining ad hoc strings.

A fourth use case is integration with automated or closed-loop laboratories. Local catalysis data infrastructures already connect automated acquisition, standardized analysis, and database upload, while autonomous experimentation literature points toward design--build--test--learn cycles in which machine-readable data are essential \cite{moshantaf2024advancing,weigmann2026autonomous}. Catalyst informatics makes the same point from the data-driven design perspective: predictive and optimization workflows require structured descriptions of catalyst composition, preparation, operating conditions, and performance \cite{takahashi2023catalysts}. Metadata extraction does not itself implement a closed-loop laboratory, but it can provide one of the prerequisite layers: converting heterogeneous experimental documentation into structured, grounded, reusable records.

\section{Research Gap}
\label{sec:research-gap}

The literature reviewed in this chapter shows that the central gap is not a lack of individual building blocks. Catalysis research already has FAIR infrastructure initiatives, repository concepts, metadata profiles, vocabularies, ontology surveys, knowledge graph methods, and increasingly capable text-mining and LLM extraction systems. NFDI4Cat, NFDI4Chem, Repo4Cat, NOMAD, ChemDCAT-AP, Voc4Cat, Ontologies4Cat, CatalysisIE, SPIRES, CatMiner, and recent LLM-based knowledge graph approaches all address important parts of the problem \cite{nfdi4cat2024whitepaper,steinbeck2023nfdi4chem,kushnarenko2025repo4cat,schumann2025nomad,stroemert2026chemdcat,veluvali2025bridging,behr2024ontologies4cat,zhang2022knowledge,caufield2024spires,walls2025catminer,oarga2026scientifickg}. What remains less developed is an operational workflow that connects these parts for heterogeneous experimental packages.

Existing extraction systems are strongest where the input is a scientific article or a controlled text corpus. CatalysisIE, ChemDataExtractor, CatMiner, SPIRES, and general structured extraction studies provide important patterns for article selection, entity extraction, schema population, normalization, and knowledge-base construction \cite{swain2016chemdataextractor,zhang2022knowledge,caufield2024spires,dagdelen2024structured,walls2025catminer}. Experimental data packages, however, can contain PDFs, spreadsheets, local metadata files, images, instrument exports, supplementary tables, and partially structured folders. The information needed for a metadata profile may therefore be distributed across document types and may not follow the rhetorical structure of a journal article.

A second gap concerns trust. Schema-valid output is necessary but not sufficient for scientific metadata. A generated record can satisfy JSON Schema or SHACL while still assigning a value to the wrong catalyst, confusing experimental and computational results, losing unit context, or grounding a term to an inappropriate vocabulary concept. The reviewed literature therefore points toward evidence retrieval, provenance, term grounding, validation, and human review as complementary controls rather than optional additions \cite{schilling2025text,liu2024lostmiddle,geng2023grammar,li2024structuredoutput,diaz2025knowledgegraphs}. The challenge is to combine these controls into a workflow that remains inspectable and practical.

This thesis is positioned in that operational gap. It does not propose a new catalysis ontology, a new repository, or a fully automated literature database. Instead, the following chapter develops SIMONE as a workflow that connects heterogeneous source handling, evidence-oriented extraction, metadata profile projection, vocabulary grounding, validation, and human review. The contribution is therefore not the replacement of existing standards or vocabularies, but the investigation of how they can be made usable as constraints and grounding resources in an LLM-supported metadata extraction process.
