\label{chap:background}

In order to develop a workflow that can extract metadata from arbitrary catalysis datasets and represent it in a standardised, machine-actionable form, it is necessary to understand the scientific context of the data to be extracted, as well as the technical foundations of the system that will execute the workflow.
The first section introduces the landscape of data in catalytic experiments, motivating the need for application-specific metadata standards.
After that, a short section presents metadata standards conceptually and its implementation in practice.
As these standards should align with common rules, the third section introduces semantic web technologies as a toolset for representing and connecting the data in a machine-actionable form, as well as providing a common language with which to formulate the standards.
The fourth section provides background on \acp{LLM}, as this work aims to utilize their capabilities to extract information from an unstructured, human-readable form into any specified format.

\section{Catalytic Experiments}
\label{sec:catalytic-experiments}

Catalysis refers to the acceleration of a chemical reaction by a substance that participates in the reaction but is not consumed by it.
A catalyst can lower the effective activation barrier and allow a reaction to proceed under milder or more technically useful conditions, as schematically shown in \cref{fig:catalysis-activation-energy}.
Consequently, catalysts are widely studied across application fields such as energy cost reduction, environmental recovery, chemical manufacturing, and materials science \cite{osman2024coordination}.
Catalytic experiments investigate how catalysts are prepared, activated, characterized, and used to transform reactants under defined reaction conditions.
The resulting data landscape is therefore not limited to final performance indicators, but spans the full experimental chain from catalyst synthesis to functional analysis and can be evaluated from different perspectives \cite{marshall2023digital}.

\subsection{Catalytic Data and Metadata}
\label{sec:catalytic-data-metadata}

During the life cycle of a catalyst, a precursor may be transformed into an activated material and subsequently into a spent or deactivated sample.
Each stage in this process can yield new characterisation and performance data, which must be linked to the corresponding material state \cite{mendes2025data}.
For this reason, a catalytic result such as a conversion, selectivity, yield, or reaction rate is only interpretable together with relevant metadata \cite{wulf2021unified}.
\Cref{tab:catalytic-data-landscape} summarizes this landscape by grouping representative data and metadata into material, preparation, characterization, reactor, operating, and performance-related categories.
These categories indicate which contextual links have to be preserved when heterogeneous datasets and kinetic measurements, are interpreted together \cite{mendes2025data,schumann2025nomad,stroemert2026chemdcat}.

\input{tables/catalytic_data_landscape}

Experimental metadata describe the context in which primary data were generated, processed, and interpreted.
In catalytic experiments, this includes sample provenance, instrument settings, calibration procedures, and processing choices.
Such information is not just a secondary annotation layer, but rather an integral part of the scientific evidence, because the same numerical activity value can have a different meaning when the catalyst pretreatment or analytical calibration changes \cite{wulf2021unified}.
Metadata therefore turn isolated measurements into documented experimental records and provides the basis for reproducing experiments or comparing evaluation studies across different materials, reactors, and laboratories \cite{wulf2021unified,salim2026methane}.
In automated catalyst testing, they also keep raw data, evaluated data, standard operating procedures, and database entries connected throughout the experimental workflow \cite{moshantaf2024advancing}.

\subsection{NMR Spectroscopy Data}
\label{subsec:nmr-spectroscopy-data}

\ac{NMR} is a common analytical method for characterising chemical samples and following transformations in reaction systems.
In a catalytic experiment, NMR data may provide evidence about product identity, the presence of intermediates or impurities, changes in reactant concentration, or the state of catalyst-related molecular species \cite{marshall2023digital,mendes2025data}.
Within the catalytic data landscape, NMR therefore belongs primarily to the characterisation category, while its use for quantitative reaction monitoring also links it to performance-related data \cite{mendes2025data}.

An NMR experiment typically produces a raw time-domain signal, commonly referred to as a free induction decay, which is processed into a frequency-domain spectrum.
The resulting spectrum represents signal intensity as a function of chemical shift, usually reported in parts per million relative to a reference signal.
Peak intensities are generally expressed in arbitrary units, whereas peak integrals may be normalised and interpreted as relative measures of the number or concentration of contributing nuclei under suitable acquisition conditions \cite{nmrmlSpecification}.
Rather than forming a single scalar measurement, an NMR spectrum contains multiple local signal features, including peaks, peak groups, integrals, multiplicities, coupling patterns, and background components such as noise, baseline drift, solvent signals, or raw data processing artifacts \cite{nmrmlSpecification}.

In practice, NMR datasets are often distributed as instrument-specific directory structures in which the raw signal is stored together with acquisition parameters.
They may additionally include processed spectra, exchange formats such as JCAMP-DX, peak or integration tables, and graphical previews \cite{nmrmlSpecification,steinbeck2023nfdi4chem}.
The experimentally observed result is therefore not limited to the displayed spectrum, but may comprise the raw signal, the processed spectral representation, derived signal features, and their chemical interpretation.
This makes NMR an instructive example for metadata extraction, because the relevant information includes both evaluated entities, such as peaks or assigned compounds, and the contextual information describing how the data were generated and processed \cite{nmrmlSpecification,steinbeck2023nfdi4chem}.

\section{Metadata Standards}
\label{sec:metadata-standards}

As the previous section demonstrated, primary data only become scientifically useful when they are connected to their experimental context.
Metadata standards address this need by defining how such context should be documented in a consistent, reusable way.
Conceptually, a metadata standard specifies the expected information, how it should be structured and how values should be expressed, enabling records to be interpreted beyond the laboratory in which they were created \cite{wulf2021unified,mendes2025data}.
In practice such a standard can be implemented as an application profile of a formal upper-level ontology such as the \ac{DCAT}.
Here, DCAT provides the universal structural blueprint, while the application profile provides the domain-specific rules, contraints, and terminologies required for actual data exchange \cite{w3c2022dcat,w3c2017shacl,stroemert2026chemdcat}.
That way, an application profile establishes a shared framework for reporting on the entities, activities, quantities, units, methods, instruments and provenance relations required to comprehend a dataset inside a specific domain context.
Some requirements describe the minimum information necessary for an experiment to be interpreted, compared, or reproduced.
Other requirements concern consistency, stipulating that values use stable units, controlled terms, identifiers and data types instead of uncontrolled local wording.
In this sense, a standard establishes an agreement between data producers and users regarding what constitutes sufficient context for reuse \cite{wulf2021unified,steinbeck2020nfdi4chem}.

This agreement is particularly important for automated metadata extraction.
While an extraction system can identify candidate values in files, tables, captions, or instrument exports, it still requires a target structure in which these values can be placed and validated.
The standard provides this target structure, distinguishing, for instance, whether an extracted phrase refers to a sample, an instrument, a measured quantity, a processing step or a data-generating activity.

\section{Semantic Web Technologies}
\label{sec:semantic-web-technologies}
Semantic web technologies provide the representation languages, identifiers, vocabularies and validation mechanisms that enable standards to be implemented in a machine-actionable form.
Their central idea is to move from a web of human-readable documents to a web of data whose meaning is explicitly described for computational processing \cite{jannidis2017digitalhumanities}.

\subsection{Ontologies}
\label{sec:ontologies}

Ontologies provide the abstract layer of data models in a domain.
They describe the classes, relations and axioms that define the structural framework for representing domain knowledge.
Classes are concepts or categories that group together things with the same characteristics.
They define a specific type of objects, such as a \textit{\textsuperscript{1}H nuclear magnetic resonance spectroscopy} class, as a subclass of the \textit{pulsed nuclear magnetic resonance spectroscopy} class.
Relations that are defined by an ontology describe how instances of different classes may be connected.
For example, an \textit{NMR spectrum} instance can be \textit{generated} by an \textit{\textsuperscript{1}H NMR spectroscopy} instance.
Axioms can further restrict the meaning of classes and relations by prescribing the type of entities that a relation can connect.

\Cref{fig:ontology-layer} illustrates the distinction between the ontology layer and the instance layer for a simplified NMR dataset example.
Here, the dataset that is holding NMR data for a catalyst sample is generated by a particular \textit{\textsuperscript{1}H NMR run R1}, which has the specified type \textit{\textsuperscript{1}H NMR spectroscopy}.
That run R1 generated a \textit{NMR spectrum} spectrum S1 and used deuterated chloroform (CDCl\textsubscript{3}) as a \textit{NMR solvent}.
Because the run R1 is a \textit{\textsuperscript{1}H NMR spectroscopy}, it uses 1H nuclei as the \textit{aquisition nucleus} per definition.
This \textsuperscript{1}H nucleus has a quantitative attribute \textit{chemical shift} with a defined value of 7.26 ppm, which locates the residual peak of chloroform (CHCl\textsubscript{3}) in the spectrum S1.
By linking all the instances together as described, we get this graph-like structure, where one can see that the chemical shift is the attribute of the \textsuperscript{1}H nucleus, while the solvent CDCl\textsubscript{3} acts as context under which the chemical shift is measured.

\subsection{Graph Models}
\label{sec:graph-models}

Semantic-web data are commonly represented using the \ac{RDF}.
\ac{RDF} models information as directed statements consisting of a subject, predicate, and object \cite{w3c2014rdf11concepts}.
The subject identifies the described resource, the predicate identifies the relation or attribute, and the object identifies either another resource or a literal value.
Instance data can be assigned to an ontology class through the \texttt{rdf:type} predicate, as illustrated in \cref{fig:ontology-layer}, while further predicates connect the instance to related resources or descriptive values.

Resources, classes, and predicates in RDF are generally identified using \acp{IRI}.
This allows statements originating from different systems to refer to the same concepts and relations rather than relying only on locally defined labels \cite{w3c2014rdf11concepts,huskova2025improvement}.
For readability, RDF serializations frequently abbreviate IRIs using compact identifiers consisting of a namespace prefix and a local identifier.
The examples in \cref{fig:ontology-layer} use concepts from the \ac{CHMO} and the \ac{NMRCV} to classify the data-generating activity and describe its acquisition context.

RDF is primarily intended for interoperable knowledge representation.
Its uniform statement model, globally identifiable predicates, and compatibility with vocabularies such as \ac{RDFS} and \ac{OWL} allow data from different sources to be linked and interpreted against shared semantic definitions.
RDF graphs are commonly queried using \ac{SPARQL}, which matches patterns of subject--predicate--object statements.
Their instance structure can additionally be validated using the \ac{SHACL}.
A SHACL shape may, for example, require that an instance of \textit{\textsuperscript{1}H NMR spectroscopy} is connected to an \textit{NMR solvent}, restrict a chemical-shift value to a numerical datatype, or specify the maximum number of elements for a property.
SHACL therefore evaluates whether an RDF graph conforms to an intended data structure, whereas an ontology provides the vocabulary and logical axioms used for classification, inference, and semantic interpretation \cite{w3c2017shacl}.

A property graph, on the other hand, uses a different representation model.
It consists of nodes connected by directed relationships, where nodes may have labels and both nodes and relationships may carry key--value properties.
Information that would be represented uniformly through RDF statements can therefore be distributed across node labels, relationship types, node properties, and relationship properties.
For example, an NMR run and a spectrum may be represented as labelled nodes connected by a \texttt{GENERATED} relationship, while a numerical acquisition value may be stored directly as a property of the corresponding node or relationship.

Property graphs are commonly used in graph database systems that focus on efficient traversal, operational querying, and indexed retrieval.
An index is an additional data structure that enables selected nodes, relationships, or property values to be located without scanning the complete graph.
Full-text indexes support lexical retrieval by tokenising textual properties and ranking matches based on words or phrases, whereas vector indexes store numerical vectors and retrieve entries according to their similarity in the embedding space.
Database implementations may additionally provide uniqueness constraints, property-type constraints, and other mechanisms that are not defined by the abstract property-graph model itself but need to be implemented explicitly.
Property graphs are queried using the ISO-standardised \ac{GQL}.

RDF data can be projected into a property graph by mapping RDF resources to nodes, selected RDF types to node labels, object-valued predicates to relationships, and literal-valued predicates to node or relationship properties.
This transformation is not necessarily semantics-preserving by default.
RDF predicates are globally identified resources, whereas relationship types and property keys in a property-graph database are often local schema elements.
Information concerning ontology axioms, named graphs, blank nodes, multi-valued properties, or metadata about individual statements must therefore be retained through an explicit mapping strategy if it is required after import.
Likewise, RDFS or OWL entailment is not automatically performed by a property-graph database unless corresponding inference rules are implemented separately.

The two graph models consequently serve overlapping but different purposes.
RDF is particularly suited to interoperable data exchange, ontology alignment, semantic integration, and reasoning over globally identified statements.
Property graphs are particularly suited to database-oriented traversal, application-specific graph analysis, and integration with operational indexing and retrieval mechanisms.


\section{Large Language Models}
\label{sec:large-language-models}
\acp{LLM} are systems based on layers of transformers and feed-forward neural networks that learn statistical patterns from large amounts of text \cite{zhao2026surveyllms,bommasani2021foundationmodels,vaswani2017attention}.
They use these patterns to predict the next piece of text in a sequence, and this capability can be used to generate arbitrary outputs, such as queries against a property graph or even structured RDF graphs \cite{brown2020language,dagdelen2024structured,zhang2024edc,oarga2026scientifickg}.
The following sections introduce the technical principles most relevant to understanding LLM-based systems, including transformer architectures, retrieval-augmented generation and methods for generating structured outputs.

\subsection{Tokenization}
\label{subsec:tokenization}

LLMs do not process raw text directly.
Before text enters the transformer, a tokenizer divides it into a sequence of discrete units called tokens.
Depending on the tokenizer, a token may represent a complete word, a word fragment, punctuation, digits, whitespace, or a special control symbol.
Subword tokenization allows a fixed vocabulary to represent both frequent terms as single tokens and uncommon expressions as combinations of smaller units \cite{kudo2018sentencepiece}.
This is particularly relevant for scientific text, where chemical formulas, identifiers, units, and uncommon technical terms may each be divided into several tokens.

Each token is mapped to an integer identifier, which is then used to retrieve a learned numerical vector called a token embedding.
Because an embedding alone does not identify where a token occurs in the sequence, positional information is combined with the token representation before it is processed by the transformer \cite{vaswani2017attention}.
The number of tokens produced by this process, rather than the number of words or characters in the original text, determines how much of the model's context window is occupied.

\subsection{Context Processing}
\label{subsec:context-processing}

The embedded token sequence is processed by a stack of transformer blocks.
Within each block, self-attention allows the representation of a token to incorporate information from other tokens in the sequence.
For this purpose, the model derives query, key, and value vectors from each token representation.
The query of one position is compared with the keys of other accessible positions, and the resulting attention weights determine how their value vectors are combined.
In a generative decoder model, a causal mask restricts this operation to the preceding tokens.
Multi-head attention performs several such operations using different learned projections, allowing the model to represent different interaction patterns simultaneously \cite{vaswani2017attention}.

\Cref{fig:self-attention} illustrates the self-attention mechanism and shows how each attention operation is followed by a position-wise feed-forward network that applies learned nonlinear transformations to every token representation.
While attention exchanges information between sequence positions, the feed-forward layers provide additional representational capacity for transforming the information available at each position.
During training, the parameters of both components are adjusted to encode statistical patterns present in the training data.
By repeatedly applying attention and feed-forward transformations, successive blocks construct increasingly context-dependent token representations.

These computations consist predominantly of large matrix operations and are therefore commonly executed on \acp{GPU}, which can perform many numerical operations in parallel.
For efficient execution, model weights, intermediate activations, and attention-related data must normally be available in the GPU's \ac{VRAM}.
System \ac{RAM} can be used through offloading, but repeatedly transferring data between RAM and the GPU introduces substantial latency and generally reduces processing speed.

The context window specifies the maximum combined number of input tokens that the model can process.
Its size is restricted by both the model architecture and computational resources.
In the standard attention formulation, processing an input sequence requires comparisons between pairs of token positions, causing the computational cost to grow quadratically with the sequence length \cite{vaswani2017attention}.
During autoregressive generation, previously calculated key and value vectors are commonly retained in a key--value cache so that they do not have to be recomputed for every new token.
The size of this cache grows with the sequence length and can consume a substantial fraction of the available VRAM \cite{kwon2023pagedattention}.
Longer contexts therefore increase processing time and memory consumption even when memory-efficient attention implementations are used.

\subsection{Output Generation}
\label{subsec:output-generation}

After the input context has been processed inside the transformer layers as shown in \cref{fig:self-attention}, an LLM maps the representation at the latest sequence position to a score for every token in its vocabulary.
These scores are converted into a probability distribution.
A decoding strategy then selects the next token.
Decoding parameters such as temperature and nucleus-sampling thresholds modify this selection process and consequently influence the variability of the generated output \cite{holtzman2019curious}.
A temperature parameter which is set to 0 selects the token with the highest probability, whereas sampling-based strategies may select alternative tokens according to their probabilities.

The selected token is appended to the sequence, after which the model predicts the following token.
This autoregressive procedure continues until an end token, a length limit, or another stopping condition is reached.
The output is therefore generated as a sequence of individual token decisions rather than as a complete data structure.
Even when the prompt requests \ac{JSON}, \ac{RDF}, or another formal representation, ordinary decoding does not inherently guarantee valid syntax, the presence of required fields, or the correct assignment of extracted values.

A structured extraction workflow must consequently distinguish between generation, parsing, and validation.
The generated text can first be passed to a strict parser and subsequently checked against a schema or constraint model.
If parsing fails, fallback strategies may isolate the intended output block, correct unambiguous syntactic defects, or repeat generation while providing the detected validation error.
When supported by the inference system, grammar-constrained decoding can prevent the generation of tokens that would violate a specified formal grammar and thereby guarantee syntactic conformity \cite{geng2023grammar}.
Such constraints do not, however, guarantee semantic correctness: a syntactically valid result may still contain unsupported values, incorrect relations, or omitted information \cite{li2024structuredoutput,dagdelen2024structured}.
A workflow should therefore not just accept syntactically valid output, but also aim to build in a semantic validation loop.

\subsection{Retrieval-Augmented Generation}
\label{subsec:retrieval-augmented-generation}

The information encoded in a language model's parameters is determined during training and cannot be updated afterwards.
At the same time, the finite context window prevents all potentially relevant knowledge from being supplied in every request.
\ac{RAG} addresses these limitations by combining the model's parametric knowledge with an external, updateable information source \cite{lewis2020retrieval,gao2023ragsurvey}.

In a typical RAG workflow, documents are divided into passages and stored in a searchable index.
For a given request, a retrieval component identifies a limited set of relevant passages using lexical matching, vector similarity, or a combination of both.
The retrieved passages may be reranked and are then inserted into the model's prompt together with the task instructions and input data \cite{gao2023ragsurvey,asai2024selfrag}.
The language model consequently generates its output using both its learned parameters and the information supplied at inference time.

RAG does not increase the model's context window.
Instead, it reduces the amount of information that must be included by selecting passages that are expected to be relevant.
This allows ontology definitions, documentation, or domain-specific examples to be incorporated without retraining the language model.
For metadata extraction, retrieval can provide definitions of target properties, valid terminology, representation examples, or documentation for an encountered file format.

The quality of the final result remains dependent on the retrieval stage.
Relevant information may be omitted because of imprecise queries or limitations of the retrieval system, while irrelevant passages may consume context capacity and misdirect generation.
RAG-based systems should therefore evaluate retrieval quality separately from generation quality and retain references to the retrieved passages so that generated extraction results can be traced to their supporting context \cite{gao2023ragsurvey,asai2024selfrag}.
